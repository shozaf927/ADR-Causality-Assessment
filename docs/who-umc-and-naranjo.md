# Individual-Case Causality Assessment: WHO–UMC and Naranjo

## 1. Why use an individual-case method?

A suspected ADR report often contains incomplete information and competing explanations. Structured methods help reviewers make their reasoning transparent and consistent. They do not establish absolute proof and should be interpreted alongside clinical judgement and applicable guidance.

## 2. WHO–UMC causality assessment

The WHO–UMC system classifies the likelihood of a causal relationship using clinical-pharmacological considerations, chronology, dechallenge, and alternative explanations.

| Category | General interpretation |
|---|---|
| Certain | A plausible time relationship; the event cannot reasonably be explained by disease or other drugs; response to withdrawal is clinically plausible; and the event is definitive pharmacologically or phenomenologically. Rechallenge, if necessary, is satisfactory. |
| Probable/Likely | A reasonable time relationship; disease or other drugs are unlikely explanations; and response to withdrawal is clinically reasonable. Rechallenge is not required. |
| Possible | A reasonable time relationship, but disease or other drugs could also explain the event; withdrawal information may be unclear or absent. |
| Unlikely | The timing makes a causal relationship improbable and another explanation is more likely. |
| Conditional/Unclassified | More data are essential for a proper assessment, or additional data are being examined. |
| Unassessable/Unclassifiable | The information is insufficient or contradictory and cannot be supplemented or verified. |

Use the official WHO–UMC guidance for the complete operational criteria. Do not assign a category merely because a reaction is listed in product information.

## 3. Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale

Naranjo et al. (1981) proposed a ten-question algorithm. Each answer receives a prescribed score; the total is interpreted as follows:

| Total score | Naranjo classification |
|---:|---|
| 9 or higher | Definite |
| 5–8 | Probable |
| 1–4 | Possible |
| 0 or lower | Doubtful |

The ten questions address:
1. Previous conclusive reports of the reaction.
2. Whether the event appeared after the suspected medicine was given.
3. Whether the reaction improved after withdrawal or an antagonist was given.
4. Whether the reaction reappeared after re-administration.
5. Whether alternative causes could independently have caused the reaction.
6. Whether the reaction reappeared with placebo.
7. Whether toxic drug concentrations were detected.
8. Whether the reaction worsened with increased dose or improved with reduced dose.
9. Whether the patient had a similar reaction to the same or a similar medicine previously.
10. Whether objective evidence confirmed the reaction.

**Scoring caution:** Use the original published questionnaire and its exact answer-specific scoring when calculating a total. Do not guess scores from a narrative summary. If an item is unknown, score it according to the instrument's instructions rather than treating missing data as a negative finding.

## 4. Comparison

| Feature | WHO–UMC | Naranjo |
|---|---|---|
| Format | Clinical judgement categories | Ten-item point-based algorithm |
| Output | Six categories | Four probability bands |
| Main inputs | Timing, dechallenge, alternatives, pharmacology | Structured questions, including dechallenge and rechallenge |
| Strength | Flexible clinical-pharmacological assessment | Transparent and reproducible questionnaire |
| Limitation | Requires trained judgement; some subjectivity | May be less suitable for complex cases, interactions, and certain clinical contexts |

The WHO–UMC and Naranjo systems are not interchangeable. Report which method was used and retain the reasoning behind the conclusion.

## 5. Worked-case discipline

For every case:
- distinguish facts from assumptions;
- document missing information;
- consider alternative causes;
- do not invent dechallenge, rechallenge, laboratory results, or previous reports;
- avoid deliberate rechallenge solely to obtain a score;
- state the method and the limitations of the assessment.

## 6. Reference

Naranjo CA, Busto U, Sellers EM, et al. A method for estimating the probability of adverse drug reactions. *Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics*. 1981;30(2):239–245. doi:10.1038/clpt.1981.154.
