# Causality Considerations in Suspected ADR Assessment

## 1. Purpose

Causality assessment evaluates how likely it is that a medicinal product contributed to an observed adverse reaction. An adverse event that occurs during treatment is not automatically an adverse drug reaction. Timing, clinical features, pharmacology, alternative causes, and follow-up evidence all matter.

## 2. Bradford Hill considerations

Sir Austin Bradford Hill described considerations for evaluating causal relationships, especially in epidemiological research. Selected considerations can structure clinical reasoning, but they should not be converted into an unvalidated patient-level score.

### Temporality

Exposure must precede the suspected reaction. Compare:
- treatment start and stop dates;
- dose changes and administration times;
- reaction onset and progression;
- known pharmacological or immunological latency.

A reaction that clearly predates exposure is unlikely to have been initiated by that exposure. A plausible sequence alone does not prove causation.

### Biological plausibility

Ask whether known pharmacology, toxicology, metabolism, immune mechanisms, or published safety evidence could explain the event. A plausible mechanism supports an association but is not proof; absence of a fully understood mechanism does not automatically exclude causality.

### Dose-response relationship

Assess whether reaction probability or severity changes with dose, concentration, or duration. Consider actual exposure, dose escalation, organ function, interactions, and therapeutic drug monitoring when relevant.

Not all reactions are dose-dependent. Immune-mediated allergy and some idiosyncratic reactions may not show a predictable dose-response relationship.

### Consistency

Consider whether similar associations have been observed repeatedly in independent studies, populations, or clinical settings. Review the quality and limitations of the evidence rather than counting reports alone.

**Do not confuse consistency with rechallenge.** Consistency is reproducibility of an association across observations. Rechallenge means recurrence after re-administration to the same patient and is a separate causality consideration.

## 3. Other useful clinical considerations

### Dechallenge

Dechallenge describes what happens after the suspected medicine is reduced or withdrawn. Improvement may support a relationship when the expected pharmacology and time course fit. Lack of improvement does not always exclude causality: the reaction may be irreversible, prolonged, or unrelated to ongoing exposure.

### Rechallenge

Rechallenge describes recurrence after the medicine is restarted. It may provide evidence, but it can expose a patient to serious harm. It must never be undertaken solely to establish causality when clinically unjustified or unsafe. Record only information from a rechallenge that occurred as part of appropriate clinical care.

### Alternative explanations

Evaluate:
- underlying disease or disease progression;
- infection or other acute illness;
- concomitant prescription and non-prescription medicines;
- herbal products, supplements, and other exposures;
- medication errors, overdose, interactions, and product-quality issues.

## 4. Practical reasoning table

| Consideration | Question | Evidence to capture |
|---|---|---|
| Temporality | Did exposure precede the reaction? | Start, stop, dose-change and onset dates |
| Plausibility | Can the product explain the clinical event? | Mechanism, known reaction profile, investigations |
| Dose-response | Did exposure changes track with reaction changes? | Dose, concentration, duration, clinical course |
| Consistency | Is the association independently observed? | Reliable literature and safety evidence |
| Dechallenge | What happened after withdrawal or reduction? | Action date and subsequent course |
| Rechallenge | Did it recur after re-exposure? | Existing clinical record; never provoke for proof |
| Alternatives | Could another cause explain the event? | Disease history, co-medications, tests |

## 5. Key limitation

Bradford Hill considerations support structured causal reasoning at a broader level. For an individual suspected ADR, use an appropriate clinical causality approach, such as WHO–UMC or the Naranjo algorithm, while recognising that neither removes uncertainty or replaces clinical judgement.
