## 1. Case summary

A fictional 35-year-old woman weighing 62 kg is prescribed oral amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for a bacterial infection. A generalised skin rash is reported on Day 3 after treatment begins.

## 3. Causality considerations

### Temporality
The rash is reported after amoxicillin initiation. This supports temporal compatibility, but the exact onset time and clinical latency are unavailable.

### Biological plausibility
Amoxicillin can be associated with hypersensitivity reactions, including cutaneous eruptions. The description is too limited to identify the rash phenotype or mechanism.

### Dose-response
No dose escalation, concentration data, or relationship between dose and reaction severity is provided. Dose-response cannot be assessed.

### Consistency
Cutaneous reactions are recognised with amoxicillin exposure. This background knowledge supports plausibility but does not establish causation in this fictional case.

### Alternative explanations
The underlying infection, viral illness, other medicines, and unrelated dermatological causes must be considered. The case provides insufficient information to evaluate them.

### Dechallenge/rechallenge
Neither is documented. Rechallenge should not be performed solely to prove causality, particularly where a hypersensitivity reaction is suspected.

## 4. Preliminary interpretation

The available facts support a **suspected association** because the rash followed treatment initiation and a cutaneous reaction is pharmacologically plausible. However, the case is incomplete. It is not scientifically appropriate to claim definite causality or assign a confident WHO–UMC category without further clinical details and evaluation of alternative causes.

## 5. Information needed for follow-up

- Exact treatment and reaction dates/times.
- Rash morphology, distribution, severity, mucosal involvement, and progression.
- Presence or absence of fever, facial swelling, breathing difficulty, or other systemic features.
- Relevant infection diagnosis and clinical course.
- All concomitant prescription, OTC, herbal, and supplement exposures.
- Allergy history and prior beta-lactam exposure.
- Examination and relevant investigations.
- Clinical action taken and subsequent course.
- Recovery status and date, if recovered.

## 6. Learning points

1. A temporal sequence is necessary but not sufficient for causality.
2. A recognised adverse reaction increases plausibility but does not prove the medicine caused this event.
3. Alternative causes and concomitant exposures must be assessed.
4. Missing data should remain explicitly unknown.
5. Rechallenge must not be used casually or solely to obtain causality evidence.
6. A high-quality safety case depends on clear chronology, clinical description, and follow-up.
